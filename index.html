☼The Sunday Spotlight☼

What a Reassessment Actually Does to Your Tax Bill
Every property in North Tonawanda is about to get a new number on it, and Dunkirk is in the middle of its own assessment fight. Here is what all of that means, for people who have never had to think about it before.
What is happening, in both cities. North Tonawanda's council voted 4–1 to reassess all 14,600 parcels and hired GAR Associates for $550,000 to do it. The last full look was 2009; the average house here is assessed at $291,000 today. Ninety minutes down the lake, Dunkirk has been arguing about the same thing from the other end — a court ruled this week in favor of city assessor Erica Munson. Two cities, one problem: what a property is worth on paper versus what it would actually sell for.
The thing almost everyone gets wrong. A reassessment is not a tax increase. The city decides separately how much money it needs — that is the levy, and it is set in the budget. The assessments only decide how that total gets divided up. When every assessment goes up, the tax rate comes down to match, because the same pile of money is being collected from bigger-looking numbers. If every property in the city rose by exactly the same percentage, nobody's bill would change at all.
So why does anyone care? Because properties never move by the same percentage. Seventeen years is a long time, and neighborhoods drift apart. In a reassessment, the winners and losers are decided by how far your property moved relative to everyone else's. Go up less than the city average and your bill goes down. Go up more and your bill goes up. The commercial numbers show how wide that spread can be: the Walmart on Niagara Falls Boulevard is currently assessed at $224,000 a year and could land at just over a million. Every dollar that shifts onto a parcel like that is a dollar that comes off the houses.
Why cities let it go so long. Because it is politically miserable. Somebody's bill always goes up, and that person calls. The cost of waiting is that the drift compounds — and a resident who has been quietly overpaying since 2009 has been subsidizing a neighbor the whole time without either of them knowing it.
What to actually do. When the notice arrives, it will list a proposed assessment, not a tax bill; do not multiply it by the old rate and panic. Check whether the number is roughly what the house would sell for today — that is the only question that matters. If it is too high, there is an informal review with the assessor before anything is final, and that conversation is far easier than the formal grievance that comes later. Bring comparable sales. Grievance Day in New York is the fourth Tuesday in May.
Worth watching both. Dunkirk is the argument after the fact; North Tonawanda is the argument about to start.

☑The Ballot☑

The Three House Seats
Forty-four days. Yesterday's paper covered the governor's race and the A.I. ads; today, the three congressional seats this family votes in. All 26 of New York's House seats are up, along with all 213 in the state legislature, the Attorney General and the Comptroller.
NY-26 — North Tonawanda. Tim Kennedy, Democrat, defending against Republican Dennis Hannon, a software engineer and former bank executive. Buffalo-centered and heavily Democratic; not on anyone's list of competitive races.
NY-23 — Dunkirk. Nick Langworthy, the Republican incumbent and former state party chairman, against Democrat Aaron Gies, a college professor. The 23rd is the most Republican district in New York.
NY-25 — Scottsville. Joe Morelle, Democrat, against Monroe County legislator Virginia McIntyre. Morelle has held the Rochester seat since 2018.
None of the three is expected to be close, which is the honest thing to say and also the reason the statewide races matter more here than the congressional ones. The competitive New York seat is in the Hudson Valley — Mike Lawler against Cait Conley — one of roughly twenty toss-ups nationally that decide the House.
Early voting Oct 24–Nov 1 · register by Oct 24 · Election Day Nov 3.
