The Lead

What the Data Centers Put in the Air

The 403 biggest data centers in the United States, the warehouse-size buildings full of computers that run the internet and A.I., give off an estimated 25.7 million metric tons of carbon dioxide a year, almost as much as Bolivia or Denmark. That is the finding of a study published Monday in the journal Nature Sustainability by a team led by Harvard researchers, The New York Times reports. It is the first peer-reviewed estimate since the A.I. boom began.

The centers used almost 2 percent of the country's electricity, more than New York City and Los Angeles combined, and produced about half a percent of its carbon dioxide. Fifty-four percent of their power came from burning fossil fuels, mostly natural gas and coal. Virginia, Iowa and Ohio account for about half the total, and Virginia alone for more than a quarter.

No public list of data centers exists, so the researchers pieced one together from a commercial dataset, building outlines and satellite images. The lead author, Gianluca Guidi, wants the federal government to require large data centers to report their electricity use and emissions. The figure is probably low: it leaves out hundreds of sites the team could not verify, and at least 250 more have opened since the study period ended.

There is pushback. Andy Masley, an independent researcher, says whether the emissions are an acceptable cost depends on how useful A.I. proves, and that part of the reason for them is how many people use it. Voters, for their part, limited or blocked data-center development in 40 of 41 local ballot measures this month, the Times reports.

The AI Editor's read (an AI wrote this paragraph, and it runs in data centers like these, so weigh it accordingly): The counter-argument is half right. A growing share of emissions is not a verdict by itself, about half a percent of the country's emissions for something a great many people use is not obviously out of line, and "is it worth it" is the right question for any industry. But it answers a different question. The study's main complaint is secrecy, and nobody can weigh a cost against a benefit when the cost had to be pieced together from satellite photos. Heavy use does not explain the fuel mix, either: the fossil share comes from where the centers were built and what powers them, and the study found Oregon's emit less carbon per unit of electricity because more of their power is fossil-free. And the costs and benefits land on different people. Users everywhere get the benefit; the towns near the plants and the people sharing that grid carry the cost, which is the backdrop to those ballot results. Waiting to see whether A.I. proves useful is reasonable as far as it goes. It is a weak reason to hold off on disclosure or cleaner power in the meantime.

Bank Apps Go Down, Then Come Back

Customers of JPMorgan Chase, Bank of America, U.S. Bank, Capital One, Truist and Wells Fargo reported trouble getting into their accounts Monday, and so did users of the payment app Zelle, according to the outage tracker Downdetector, CBS reports. The tracker showed service back to normal early Tuesday; Chase said its problem was a technical bug, not a cyberattack.

Ukraine Knocks Out the Crimea Bridge

The bridge linking Russia to occupied Crimea is closed after a Ukrainian strike sent a section of it into the water, the BBC reports; Russian officials confirmed drones had damaged it. Kyiv's mayor said two important bridges over the Dnipro River in the capital were closed after Russian attacks.

Amherst · A Wayfair Store for the Old Boulevard Mall

Wayfair, the online furniture seller, plans a store of about 96,000 square feet at 1261 Niagara Falls Boulevard, on the site of the former Boulevard Mall, the company announced. It is expected to open in 2028 and would be Wayfair's 12th. It will go into the space that last held Gabe's and, in the mall's heyday, Sears, WIVB reports; Supervisor Shawn Lavin said the rest of the mall will come down, and the town, which is in court to take the property by eminent domain, hopes to have it by early December and hand it back to Benderson Development for a mixed-use project that includes housing. Benderson's Eric Recoon called the store the "exciting first step."

What will be inside: Wayfair promises a curated selection, design experts on site, many items to take home the same day and bigger purchases delivered. The best guide is its existing stores, though a guide is not a promise. The first, in Wilmette, Illinois, shows furniture in room settings grouped by style, with a walled-off mattress shop, a home-project area with appliances, cabinets, tile and working showers and faucets, a free design studio, housewares, rugs, lighting, outdoor, kids and pets, and a cafe by the door, according to The Record North Shore. Shelf tags carry codes to scan, and screens open the full online catalog. In Atlanta, roughly whatever fits in the car goes home with you; sofas are not stocked and ship from a warehouse, Axios reports.

Amherst's store is about two-thirds the size of those two, so expect a trimmed-down version. Not announced: the number of floors, the exact opening date, the number of jobs, and whether Amherst gets the cafe.

Against IKEA: both are big home stores with food. Wayfair sells many brands and styles and adds home improvement, shoppers wander between departments instead of following one path, and there is no warehouse hall of flat boxes. IKEA gets here first: it has said it will open a store of about 65,000 square feet at Walden Galleria in early 2027.

2

Rochester · Cheerleading Is Back, as One Squad

Competitive cheerleading returns to Rochester city schools this winter as a single district-wide squad, not separate school teams, WXXI reports. The district had cut the funding this year and faced a public backlash; Superintendent Eric Jay Rosser announced the new format at a school board meeting last week.

3

Chautauqua County · More Patrols on the Rail Trails

The Chautauqua County Sheriff's Office is stepping up patrols on the Chautauqua Rails-to-Trails system after reports of motorized vehicles damaging the paths, Sheriff James Quattrone said, according to WKBW. The system runs nearly 26 miles along old railroad beds from Sherman north to Brocton.

The Name Is Pattinson

Robert Pattinson is the next James Bond, the franchise announced Monday, ending the long guessing game over who would follow Daniel Craig, CBS reports. He will be the seventh actor in the role since Dr. No in 1962, and called it "the honour of a lifetime."

2

Thirteen Years of Waiting for a Video Game

Fans of Grand Theft Auto 6, the first new entry in the series in 13 years, are booking time off work, buying consoles and even pretending to be in New Zealand to get in early, the BBC reports. One research firm, Sensor Tower, estimates 12 million advance orders worth $1.18 billion, a figure the game's maker has not confirmed.

3

Oprah's List Turns 30

Oprah Winfrey has put out her Favorite Things list, the holiday gift guide she started on her talk show in 1996, the Hollywood Reporter says. It began by accident, the magazine recounts, when a producer gave her a pair of pajamas she liked so much that she gave a pair to everyone in her studio audience.
