Starship Comes Home, and the Tower Catches It

SpaceX caught its Starship spacecraft out of the sky Saturday. After about 22 hours in orbit, the top half of the world's biggest rocket came back through the heat of reentry, flipped upright over its Texas launch site, and settled into two giant mechanical arms on the launch tower, CBS reports. The BBC notes the arms are nicknamed "chopsticks."

It was the first time a Starship has been recovered from orbit; earlier test flights ended with planned splashdowns in the ocean. The other half of the plan did not work: the attempt to catch the booster, the bottom part of the rocket, was called off minutes after Friday's launch.

"One of the most incredible and historic engineering feats of all time has just happened right in front of our eyes."
— Jessie Anderson, SpaceX commentator

Why it matters: a rocket that can be caught and flown again is far cheaper than one thrown away after each trip. SpaceX has to recover both halves reliably before that pays off, the BBC says, and founder Elon Musk says the goal is cheaper launches to orbit, the moon and, eventually, Mars.

The Sunday Spotlight

This Winter's Heating Bill

An AI wrote this section.

What the forecast says. National Grid, the company that delivers power to much of upstate New York, put out its winter forecast last month, covering Nov. 1 through March 31. A typical home heating with natural gas (713 therms, the unit gas is billed in) should pay about $9 less than last winter. The electric bill goes the other way: up 3 to 5 percent, or $22 to $39 over the five months, depending on where you live, CNY Central reports. Both numbers assume an ordinary winter.

Why the bill has two parts. A bill has two parts. Supply is the gas or electricity itself, passed along at cost. Delivery is the pipes, wires and crews. Delivery charges are rising under a three-year rate plan the state approved in August 2025, which includes replacing about 112 miles of leak-prone gas pipe, FingerLakes1 reports. Even so, the company expects the typical gas bill to come in slightly lower.

The fuel that did jump. Heating oil. The state's energy agency listed it at $6.21 a gallon in early October, up 67 percent from $3.72 a year earlier. Propane was up about 13 percent. Anyone with a tank in the yard is facing a very different winter than anyone on a gas line.

What it means here. One catch for this family: National Grid sells gas mainly in central and eastern New York. In most of Western New York the gas comes from National Fuel, and around Rochester from RG&E, so the $9 figure shows which way gas bills are heading, not what a house here will pay. The electric forecast is the one that applies where National Grid delivers the power: plan on $22 to $39 more over the winter. Either way, the weather will matter more than the rates. NYSEG and RG&E, which serve parts of Western New York and the Rochester area, have not put out a comparable winter number that we could find; last February they said supply prices, the cost of the energy itself, were up more than 44 percent for NYSEG customers and nearly 36 percent for RG&E customers from a year before, so their customers have reason to watch the supply line on the bill.

The cheap fixes, by National Grid's own figures. Each degree you turn the thermostat down may save 1 to 3 percent a year on heating. Sealing drafts may save about 15 percent on heating and cooling. Change the furnace filter monthly in heating season. These are the utility's estimates, and they are modest: none of them makes a big bill small.

Help paying. HEAP, the state's Home Energy Assistance Program, which gives grants toward heating bills, opened for the season Nov. 2. The income limit is $3,563 a month for one person, $4,659 for two and $6,852 for a family of four; apply through the county social services office. National Grid also has a budget plan that spreads the year's cost over 12 even payments.

Coming down the road. The rate plan runs three years, so delivery charges are likely to keep climbing after this winter. And the forecast was made with prices from the end of September; a hard cold snap can still change the supply side.

Update: A Wiring Job Stopped the Flights

Friday's nationwide ground stop traced back to work replacing old copper wire with fiber-optic cable in the air traffic control system, Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy said, according to the BBC; he said the government is trying to learn whether the contractor, L3Harris, or faulty equipment was to blame. The disruption carried into Saturday as airlines worked through the backlog.

Measles Is No Longer "Eliminated" Here

The United States has lost its status as a country where measles is eliminated, a label it had held for a quarter century, because outbreaks are no longer ending within a year, NPR reports, citing researchers who told KFF Health News of the Pan American Health Organization's decision. More than 8,000 people have been sickened and more than 830 hospitalized in the past two years; two doses of the measles vaccine prevent about 97 percent of infections.

Zelenskyy: U.S. Asked About Land for NATO

Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy said American negotiators asked him for the first time whether Ukraine would pull out of Donbas, its contested eastern region, in exchange for membership in NATO, the Western military alliance, CBS reports. He said that if a full package of security guarantees, troops, weapons and money were offered, he would put the question to Ukrainians.

The Voice at the Bottom of "Elvira"

Richard Sterban, the bass singer who sang the "oom poppa, mow mow" in the Oak Ridge Boys' hit "Elvira," died Saturday at 83, Billboard reports. He started out singing backup for Elvis Presley and retired from touring last year.
