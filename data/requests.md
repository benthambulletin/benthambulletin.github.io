- **Sun Oct 11, 2026 — Spotlight addition (Garret, Oct 10): AI in weather forecasting, tied to lake effect.** Garret pasted the New York Times piece by Judson Jones (Oct 2026) on AI models during Hurricane Isaias. Paraphrase only; credit "The New York Times" in the text. Key points from it: the physics models first pointed Isaias west toward Alabama and Mississippi while the experimental AI models pushed it east toward Pensacola; the physics models drifted toward the AI models through the week, and landfall came just over 40 miles east of Pensacola, near Destin. The Hurricane Center's Jamie Rhome says forecasters don't pick a "winning horse" but blend models with human judgment, which beats any one model; AI models now earn trust on storm tracks ("you can't call it luck after you're skillful so many times") but still trail physics models on strength. A Google DeepMind paper (August) found AI beat widely used models across 2023–25 storms; Melissa's record intensity was called partly thanks to it. Misses: both AI and physics models missed Otis (2023), which blew up where there are few sensors. Rhome: humans aren't being replaced; "our role as trusted communicators will only increase." **Lake-effect tie (say plainly it's the open question, don't overclaim):** lake-effect bands are often only a few miles wide and set by the lake-air temperature gap and the wind direction over Erie and Ontario; most AI weather models run on a grid of roughly 25 kilometers (about 15 miles), coarser than a single band, so for where the band sets up the Buffalo office still leans on fine-grained physics models and its own forecasters. AI may help with the bigger picture (when the cold air arrives, how long the wind holds), which is half the call. Verify the grid-size figure before printing. Fit it into the lake-effect Spotlight as its own beat (what happened / what it means for us / what's next) and the "Coming down the road" close.
