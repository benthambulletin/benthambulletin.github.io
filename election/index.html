What the words mean

Plain-English guide to the numbers above
Generic ballot
A poll question that names no candidates: "Would you vote for the Democrat or the Republican for Congress in your district?" Averaged nationally, it is the best single read on which way the House is leaning. D +9 means Democrats lead by about 9 points. For scale, 2018's blue wave was about D +8 and Democrats gained 41 seats; 2022 was roughly R +3 and Republicans barely took the House.
Net approval
The share of adults who approve of the president minus the share who disapprove. Negative means more people disapprove. Presidents with net approval this low usually lose seats in a midterm.
Toss Up, Lean, Likely, Solid
Race ratings from the Cook Political Report, a nonpartisan handicapper. Toss Up means either side could win; Lean is a slight edge; Likely a clear one; Solid is not in doubt. Ratings move when polls or candidates change.
Odds of a Democratic Senate
Prediction markets (Polymarket, Kalshi) let people bet real money on outcomes; the price is the crowd's probability. 64% means the market thinks Democrats win the Senate about two times in three. It is a forecast, not a poll.
Why +4
The Senate is 53 Republicans to 47 Democrats (counting two independents who vote with Democrats). Democrats need a net gain of four seats for 51. With Georgia, Michigan and Minnesota to defend, that means winning four of the six Republican-held toss-ups — Maine, Ohio, Texas, Iowa, Kansas, Alaska.
Average vs. one poll
The headline number for each Senate race is RealClearPolling's average of recent polls, which is steadier than any single survey. The note under it lists the latest individual polls. "Republican-leaning" or "Democratic-leaning" after a pollster means the firm works for that party or has a record of tilting that way — weigh those lighter.
Margin of error
Every poll is a sample. A typical one is accurate to about 3–5 points either way, so a 2-point lead is a coin flip and even a 6-point lead can flip. Averages of several polls are steadier than any one poll.
The bars
In the Senate section the bar runs from the middle: blue to the left for a Democratic lead, red to the right for a Republican one, longer for a bigger lead. In the governor section the two bars run toward each other and the gap is undecided voters.

What changed since yesterday

  • Iowa: Marist (Oct 8–11) has Turek 48–45, the second straight poll with the Democrat ahead after YouGov's 47–46; RealClearPolling's average is now Turek +0.9. The four pickups still run through Maine, Ohio and Texas plus one of Iowa, Kansas and Alaska — and Iowa just got a little easier.
  • Kansas: RealClearPolling's average lands on an exact tie, 45.8–45.8. The polls showing Hamilton ahead (InsiderAdvantage +1, YouGov +3) are offset by NYT/Siena (Marshall +1) and a September co/efficient poll (Marshall +5). Still a toss-up either way.
  • Ohio: the average is Brown +3.1, but a Republican-leaning co/efficient poll (Oct 5–6) has Husted up 1, the first poll with Husted ahead since June. CNN's Brown +6 remains the latest nonpartisan read.
  • Minnesota: KSTP/SurveyUSA (Oct 15) has Flanagan 45–42 over Tafoya for the open Democratic seat; RealClearPolling counts it a toss-up, Cook Likely D. It is a hold Democrats cannot afford to lose on the way to 51.
  • New York: a Pollfinity text-message poll (Oct 8–12, 499 RV) has Hochul 47–44, far below Marist's 11 and Emerson's 12. The firm is self-funded by a 2024 Libertarian presidential candidate and the sample is small; the averages barely moved. Cook also moved NY-04 (Gillen) from Lean D to Likely D on Oct 9.
  • Generic ballot: Silver Bulletin slipped from D+8.8 to D+8.6; RealClearPolling from D+8.1 to D+7.9. Trump's approval is unchanged at −21.3 (RCP).

What could still change this

The October surprise already happened, in February: the Iran war. It is the main reason the president's net approval sits near −21 instead of the −10 range he held at New Year's, and nothing in the next three weeks is likely to swing the national mood that far again. So the question is not whether a shock changes the House — at D+8.6 that would take a very large one — but whether anything tips the four to six Senate seats sitting inside the margin of error.

  • The war itself: A ceasefire, a major casualty event or an escalation is the one thing that could move national numbers several points in either direction. Everything else is small by comparison.
  • Prices at the pump: Gas is the number voters feel directly. A spike in the last two weeks hurts the party in power; a drop helps a little.
  • A candidate moment: Texas and Maine are within two points, so one bad week for a candidate — a scandal, a debate stumble — can decide a seat.
  • The early vote: Early voting starts Oct 24. The first week's turnout in the toss-up states is the first real signal; prediction markets react to it before the polls do.

What usually matters less than it looks: a late rally blitz (it fires up both bases) and any single surprising poll. Ignore it unless an average moves. Editor's read, Oct 15; this is judgment, not polling.
