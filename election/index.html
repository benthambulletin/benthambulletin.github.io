How to read this without panicking

  • A lead of 2 points is a tie. A lead of 5 is a real edge, not a lock. Polls miss by 3–5 points in a normal year.
  • One poll means almost nothing. The averages on this page are what to watch, and they move slowly on purpose.
  • Headlines say "Democrats worry" because worry gets clicks. Check it against the numbers here before you believe it.
  • What would actually be bad news: the averages moving 3 or more points toward Republicans across several states at once. One state wobbling is noise.
  • The Senate may not be known on election night (see Election night on the My ballot tab). A slow count is not a bad count.

Why the mood is sour

Is it real or just headlines? Three government numbers say it's real, and none of them will move before Nov 3.

  • Growth is real but narrow. Business investment is near $4 trillion a year, and almost all of the rise since 2023 is software, chips and data centers. Investment in everything else has been flat since 2022. (Bureau of Economic Analysis)
  • Nobody's getting fired, and nobody's getting hired. Layoffs are near record lows, but hiring has slid back to 2016 levels and fewer people quit than before the pandemic. People have jobs; they don't have options. (Bureau of Labor Statistics, JOLTS)
  • Prices are rising faster than paychecks again. Wages are up 3.0% over the past year; prices are up 3.7%. The lines crossed this year for the first time since 2023, so the average worker is losing ground. (Bureau of Labor Statistics, CPI and average hourly earnings)

Election night

Polls close in waves; counts finish on each state's own schedule. Here is what is normal, so a slow result doesn't read as a bad one.

  • By 10 p.m.: New York (governor, NY-17, NY-26 and the local races) and most of the East count fast. North Carolina, Georgia and Ohio usually report the bulk of their vote within a few hours of polls closing.
  • The early tell (around 11 p.m.): The four Pennsylvania seats on the House tab count fast and are the first real read on the House. Trump's approval in the state is 34% (Times/Inquirer/Siena).
  • Late night: Texas, Iowa and Kansas typically have near-complete counts by midnight Eastern. Michigan and Minnesota can run into the early morning when close.
  • Days later: Maine: if nobody clears 50% of first choices (write-in candidates make that likely), the ranked-choice count happens in Augusta and took about nine days in June's primary. Alaska: by law the ranked-choice tabulation is Nov 18 and by-mail ballots count if postmarked by Nov 3, so a close race there waits two weeks.
  • The upshot: If the Senate comes down to Maine or Alaska, control may not be known until mid-November. That is the calendar, not a sign anything went wrong.

What the words mean

Generic ballot
A poll question that names no candidates: "Would you vote for the Democrat or the Republican for Congress in your district?" Averaged nationally, it is the best single read on which way the House is leaning. D +9 means Democrats lead by about 9 points. For scale, 2018's blue wave was about D +8 and Democrats gained 41 seats; 2022 was roughly R +3 and Republicans barely took the House.
Net approval
The share of adults who approve of the president minus the share who disapprove. Negative means more people disapprove. Presidents with net approval this low usually lose seats in a midterm.
Toss-up, Lean, Likely, Solid
Race ratings from the Cook Political Report, a nonpartisan handicapper. Toss-up means either side could win; Lean is a slight edge; Likely a clear one; Solid is not in doubt. Ratings move when polls or candidates change. Sabato's Crystal Ball (University of Virginia) is a similar nonpartisan handicapper; when it differs from Cook, the race's polls note says so.
Bellwether
A race whose result tends to show how the whole night is going. This year's are the four Pennsylvania seats on the House tab.
Odds of a Democratic Senate
Prediction markets (Polymarket, Kalshi) let people bet real money on outcomes; the price is the crowd's probability. Around 65% means the market thinks Democrats win the Senate about two times in three. It is a forecast, not a poll.
Hold, pickup
A "hold" is keeping a seat your party already has; a "pickup" (or "flip") is taking one from the other party. Democrats need four more pickups than losses to reach 51 seats.
Average vs. one poll
The headline number for each Senate race is RealClearPolling's average of recent polls, which is steadier than any single survey. "Show the polls" under each race lists the latest individual polls. "Republican-leaning" or "Democratic-leaning" after a pollster means the firm works for that party or has a record of tilting that way — weigh those lighter.
Margin of error
Every poll is a sample. A typical one is accurate to about 3–5 points either way, so a 2-point lead is a coin flip and even a 6-point lead can flip. Averages of several polls are steadier than any one poll.
The bars
Each bar runs from the middle: blue to the left for a Democratic lead, red to the right for a Republican one. Longer means a bigger lead. A race with no bar has no recent public poll.

What could still change this

The national mood has been set since the Iran war began in February. What could still tip the close Senate races: a ceasefire or escalation, a gas-price spike, a bad week for a candidate in Texas or Maine, or the first week of early-vote turnout. A late rally or one surprising poll usually matters less than it looks. Judgment, not polling.
